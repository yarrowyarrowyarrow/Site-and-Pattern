# V2.97 — Birds with their own bodies

*One item: **F174**, from the V2.87 audit: twelve large birds drawn as a songbird
at their real wingspan, perched inside a plant. The owner said "Continue" after
V2.96, which offered F174 next.*

## What was wrong

Every bird in the 3D preview is one of three builds (passerine, woodpecker,
hummingbird), chosen by words in its common name. Every one is perched inside the
plant it is tied to. Measured through the real pipeline: each bird alone on a
plant it has an edge to, `build_scene` → `wildlife_for_scene` → the viewer, the
drawn box read back from the scene.

| Bird | Its plant | Drawn as | Drawn length | Real length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sandhill Crane | Broad-leaved Arrowhead | a songbird inside the plant, 0.74 m up | 3.03 m | about 1.1 m |
| Snow Goose | Saltgrass | a songbird among the grass stems | 2.22 m | about 0.7 m |
| Red-tailed Hawk | Balsam Poplar | a songbird inside the crown | 1.87 m | about 0.55 m |
| Great Horned Owl | Trembling Aspen | a songbird inside the crown | 1.76 m | about 0.55 m |
| Ruffed Grouse | Bearberry | a songbird sitting in a 15 cm mat | 0.86 m | about 0.45 m |
| American Robin | Black Hawthorn | a songbird | 0.52 m | about 0.25 m |
| Black-capped Chickadee | Chokecherry | a songbird | 0.31 m | about 0.13 m |

Three things, and the last reaches every bird, not only the big ones:

1. **One body plan.** Hawks, falcons, the owl, grouse and ptarmigan, the
   shoveler, the goose and the crane get the passerine: a round body, a round
   head, a cone beak and a slab tail.
2. **One place.** A bird is perched inside the crown of its plant, at 72% of a
   tree's height or 80% of anything shorter. A goose tied to a grass sits among
   its stems, and a grouse tied to bearberry sits in a mat 15 cm tall.
3. **Every bird is drawn at 2 to 3 times its length.** V2.46 sized a bird by
   scaling its model until its width equals the recorded wingspan. But the
   model's wings are short paddles: its whole width is 0.65 of its length, where
   a real bird's wingspan is 1.3 to 2.2 times its length. So the width that was
   set to the wingspan carries a body 1.5 times as long, and a robin with a
   34 cm wingspan is drawn 52 cm long. V2.46 checked the number it set (width
   equals wingspan) and not the length that came with it.

**How often it shows.** Over 400 random designs (the V2.88 audit's simulation,
July), the twelve are 18% of the birds placed by day and appear in **45% of
designs**. Ruffed Grouse alone is in a quarter of them, tied to bearberry, bog
blueberry, buffaloberry and wild rose. At night the Great Horned Owl is the
only bird, in 6% of designs.

## The change

### Nine builds, each at its birds' proportions

The bird file (`fauna_bird.glb`) carries nine builds:

- **Re-authored:** passerine, woodpecker, hummer.
- **New:** raptor, owl, grouse, duck, goose, crane.

Every build is authored with **wings at its real span**: the model's width, wing
tip to wing tip, is its wingspan, at the proportions its birds have.

| Build | Birds | Wingspan ÷ length | Where it stands |
|---|---|---|---|
| passerine | the songbirds, jays, magpie, dove | 1.55 | perched in the crown, as now |
| woodpecker | Downy Woodpecker, Northern Flicker | 1.75 | perched in the crown |
| hummer | the three hummingbirds | 1.35 | hovering at the flowers |
| raptor | American Kestrel, Merlin, Red-tailed Hawk | 2.2 | on a tall tree's top, upright |
| owl | Great Horned Owl | 2.1 | on a tall tree's top, upright |
| grouse | Ruffed Grouse, the three ptarmigans | 1.3 | on the ground |
| duck | Northern Shoveler | 1.6 | on the water, or the ground |
| goose | Snow Goose | 2.0 | on the water, or the ground |
| crane | Sandhill Crane | 2.0 (standing, neck up) | on the ground, about a metre tall |

The proportions are field-guide ranges for the catalogue's own species (length
and wingspan as the Cornell Lab and Sibley give them). They are drawing
constants, recalled rather than fetched in this session, like the wingspans
`bird_morphology` already carries (`verified = 0`).

**Why this fixes the size.** The size code is unchanged. It still scales a model
until its width is the recorded wingspan, and that is now true of the model. The
length follows from the build's proportions. So a robin with a 34 cm wingspan is
drawn about 22 cm long.

**The wings fold.** Long wings cannot stay spread on a perch. Each build carries
two pairs:

- `WingL`/`WingR`, spread, are the ones that beat, as before;
- `FoldL`/`FoldR` lie along the back.

The viewer shows the folded pair on a perch, walking or swimming, and the spread
pair in the air. A file without the fold nodes behaves as before.

**Upright perchers fly level.** A hawk sits at about 55° and an owl at about
70°, and both fly level. So the raptor and owl are built level, as they fly, with
the head and the owl's face turned down by about that angle. The viewer tips them
up by `perch_pitch` on a perch, where the face then looks ahead, and in the air
they look down, as hunting birds do. One number per build, in
`assetlib/bird_builds.py` (`BIRD_PERCH_PITCH`) and copied to
`src/bird_body_plan.py`; a test keeps the two equal.

**The grouse build is the ruffed grouse's.** At a span of 1.3 times its length
it draws a Ruffed Grouse 42 cm long (43-50 published). A ptarmigan's wing is
longer for its body, so the White-tailed Ptarmigan comes out about a fifth long.
The Ruffed Grouse is in a quarter of designs; the first cut, 1.4, drew it 10%
short.

**Ground birds stand on their feet.** The grouse, duck, goose and crane have
legs, and their origin is at their feet, so height 0 puts them on the ground.

### Which bird gets which build: the genus

`src/bird_body_plan.py` (new) routes a bird by its **genus**, a recorded field,
where the name words it replaces could only find woodpeckers and hummingbirds:

- *Falco*, *Buteo* → raptor
- *Bubo* → owl
- *Bonasa*, *Lagopus* → grouse
- *Anas* → duck
- *Anser* → goose
- *Grus* → crane
- *Dryobates*, *Colaptes* → woodpecker
- *Archilochus*, *Selasphorus* → hummer
- everything else → passerine

Those are the catalogue's genera. The table also names the genera beside them in
the same families that a prairie list is likely to add (*Accipiter*, *Strix*,
*Branta* and so on), so a new hawk or goose gets its body without an edit here.
The genus finds every woodpecker and hummingbird the old words did, and nothing
else.

The colour table stays as it is and moves to the new module. The goose is still
drawn brown (a Snow Goose is white) and the crane buff (a Sandhill Crane is
grey). Bird colours are F172's "21 bird colour rows", which need a source. This
changes shapes, not colours.

### Where each bird stands

Decided in Python, once, when the scene's animals are chosen:

- **crown** (passerine, woodpecker, hummer): unchanged.
- **tree** (raptor, owl):
  - They perch **on the top** of a tree they are tied to, drawn at least 3 m
    tall: their own, else another tree in the design they are tied to by any
    relationship, nearest first, and the hover label then names that tree and
    that relationship. A hawk is never in a shrub.
  - The "any relationship" was a correction. The first cut looked only at the
    ties that chose the bird, its best-ranked ones, so in a design with a White
    Spruce and a Trembling Aspen the Great Horned Owl (cover in the spruce,
    nesting in the aspen) vanished from year 1 of the timeline: the spruce is
    2.5 m then, the aspen 5 to 11 m. It now sits on the aspen, "nests in".
  - The feet are on the tip: the body's centre sits `PERCH_FOOT` × wingspan
    above it, the depth of the upright body below its centre (a test keeps the
    copy equal to `assetlib/bird_builds.perch_foot`). The tail hangs past it.
  - Two never share a top; the second soars.
  - They fly between their tree and up to three other tall trees nearby, top to
    top.
  - With none tall enough (a young design on the timeline), a raptor **soars**:
    circling at 25 m over the design's centre, wings spread.
  - An owl with no tree is not drawn. Great Horned Owls do not circle over yards,
    and drawing one where it could not be is inventing it (P9).
  - **Why the top.** The first cut perched them at 82% of the height, half the
    crown's radius out. On a poplar that is in a gap among the leaves; on a
    spruce, which has narrowed to a spire by then, it is in the air: the Merlin
    sat 1.9 m out from a trunk whose needles reach about 0.6 m there. The top is
    where hawks, falcons and owls do sit to watch, and the one point every crown
    the viewer draws has.
- **ground** (grouse, crane):
  - They stand on the ground at their plant: in the patch when it is a mat or a
    low plant (40 cm or less), at the crown's edge when it is taller, since
    nothing stands inside a shrub.
  - They walk, slowly, between their plants and a couple of others, and pause
    to feed.
- **water** (duck, goose):
  - If the design has a pond, they float on it: on the water `pond_habit` draws,
    sunk to their waterline, drifting between points on the same pond.
  - With no pond, they stand and walk like the ground birds. Snow Geese graze on
    land as much as they swim.

**What the Mourning Dove gets: nothing new.** It has no plant edge in the
catalogue, so no design can place it, and birds are never the fly-through
avatar. A dove build would be a model nobody sees. The American Kestrel has no
edge either; it gets the raptor build because its genus does, at no cost.

### The viewer

- A bird's `anim` now comes from Python: `perch`, `hover` (the hummingbirds),
  `walk` (ground and water) or `soar`.
  - **`walk`**: slow travel at the waypoint's own height (the ground, or the
    waterline), long pauses, wings folded, body level. No mammal hop.
  - **`soar`**: the flight branch with no pauses, wings spread, around the circle
    its route draws, one turn in 10-30 s. The first cut's 5 m/s went round an
    8 m circle every 6 s, which reads as frantic.
- **The perch pitch is per build** (`app.perch_pitch`; 0.45, the songbird's,
  when absent).
- **The size label names the wingspan**: "wingspan 198 cm — shown life size",
  where it said "198 cm" over a crane standing 1.1 m tall. Butterflies and moths
  say it too, since theirs is a wingspan as well.

## Left alone, on purpose

- **Bird colours** (F172), above.
- **A dove build**, above.
- **Where the twelve occur.** Ptarmigans are alpine and arctic birds, and they
  appear in 113 of the 400 simulated designs because they eat bearberry, bog
  blueberry and alpine bistort. Whether a ptarmigan belongs in a Calgary yard is
  a range question, like the plants' `site_fit`. The animals have no range data,
  and this changes how they look and stand, not which appear. Opened as **F190**.
- **Birds flying down to the ground.** Ground and water birds walk and swim; none
  takes off. Flight for them needs a landing, which is its own piece of work.
- **The flight model.** Wingbeats, bounds and pitch in the air are V2.45-V2.46's
  and do not change. Only which wings are shown does.
- **A songbird's first second on a conifer.** A songbird appears on a ring 0.3 of
  its plant's width out, at 72% of the height, which on a spruce is open air
  (rendered: two crossbills and a Pine Grosbeak beside a White Spruce). Its
  waypoints are the plant's axis at that height, so it flies into the needles
  within a second and sits there; the ring is only where it spawns. Left as it
  is: the same axis is where the hawks now sit, one crown up.

## How it is checked

- `tests/test_bird_body_plan.py` (new, 30 tests, no browser):
  - the routing by genus: every one of the 49 birds gets a build the file
    carries, the twelve get theirs, and the genus finds exactly the woodpeckers
    and hummingbirds the name words did;
  - the three copied numbers (`PERCH_PITCH`, `WATERLINE`, `PERCH_FOOT`) equal to
    the builds', and only the standing builds have legs;
  - each stance with synthetic plants: a hawk on its tree's top and only on tall
    living trees, on another of its trees when its own is young (the label
    taking that tree's relationship), soaring when it has none, two never on one
    top; an owl with no tree not drawn; a grouse in a mat and at a shrub's edge;
    every ground waypoint at 0; a goose on the pond's water at its waterline, and
    on the ground without one;
  - the same through `build_scene` and `wildlife_for_scene` from the catalogue,
    the owl on the aspen in year 1, and the size label reading "wingspan 198 cm";
  - the shipped `fauna_bird.glb`, read with its node transforms: every build with
    both pairs of wings, exactly as wide as its span, a standing build's feet at
    its origin, and fourteen birds' drawn lengths, from their recorded wingspans,
    within 15% of the field-guide range.
- `tests/test_accuracy_render.py`, three new tests over seven birds as the viewer
  builds them (`glbCritter`, `scaleCritterToLife`, folded wings): as wide as the
  recorded wingspan, drawn near the published length (the crane by its height),
  and the spread wings hidden at rest.
- The tests that asked `_bird_appearance(name)` for a build ask the new module
  with the genus; three regexes in `test_wildlife_heading.py` accept the
  `|| c.anim === 'soar'` and `app.anim || 'perch'` forms.
- **Against V2.96**, with the two new test files and the two modules they import
  copied into a V2.96 worktree, ten of the new tests fail: all four through the
  scene, all four on the model file, and two of the three probe tests. The
  routing and synthetic-placement tests pass there, since they exercise the
  copied module rather than V2.96's code. So does the probe's width test, which
  is V2.46's blind spot exactly: the width was always right.
- **Full suite** (`scripts/run_tests.py`, as CI runs it) on the first commit:
  4,007 tests, 0 failed, 7 skipped, `RESULT: OK`; after the second, the 323 tests
  of every module that touches the wildlife code. `validate-data` is clean.
- **CI**: on `d689d779`, Tests, the macOS DMG and the Windows installer all
  green. On `6dcd002e` both builds were green and Tests was cancelled by the
  second push.

## Results

**Drawn sizes**, V2.96 → V2.97. V2.96's figures are from the failing tests
above; V2.97's are from the probe, or from the model file for birds the probe
does not build.

| Bird | V2.96 | V2.97 | Field guide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black-capped Chickadee | 31 cm long | 13 cm | 12-15 cm |
| Ruby-throated Hummingbird | 17 cm | 7.5 cm | 7-9 cm |
| Downy Woodpecker | 51 cm | 16 cm | 14-18 cm |
| American Robin | 52 cm | 22 cm | 20-28 cm |
| Blue Jay | 63 cm | 26 cm | 25-30 cm |
| Northern Flicker | 91 cm | 28 cm | 28-31 cm |
| Ruffed Grouse | 86 cm | 42 cm | 43-50 cm |
| Great Horned Owl | 1.76 m | 57 cm | 46-63 cm |
| Red-tailed Hawk | 1.87 m | 57 cm | 45-65 cm |
| Snow Goose | 2.22 m | 69 cm | 69-83 cm |
| Sandhill Crane | a songbird 1.4 m tall | stands 1.07 m | about 1 m |

**Most birds come within 10% of their field-guide length, and the misses are
their tails.** One span-to-length ratio per build cannot follow a bird whose
tail is unusually long or short for its family. The magpie and the catbird are
drawn 14-15% short. The nuthatches, the Red-eyed Vireo, the House Finch and the
White-tailed Ptarmigan are 16-25% long. Before, every bird was about 2.4 times
its length (the woodpeckers 3 times). The field-guide ranges were recalled, not
fetched: the Cornell Lab's site is outside this session's network.

**Where they stand**, in renders (the before/after sheets) and in motion, with
the animator stepped 50 ms a frame for 60 s:
- the grouse walks between its plants at about 0.3 m/s, feet on the ground,
  facing the way it goes, wings folded, pausing at each plant;
- the Snow Goose drifts between four points on the pond at its waterline;
- the Red-tailed Hawk sits upright on a poplar's top with its wings folded,
  spreads them to fly to the spruce, and folds them there;
- over a young design the hawk circles at 25 m with wings spread, a turn in
  about 16 s, and never lands; the owl is not drawn.

**Over the same 400 random designs** as the survey (mature, July):
- all 45 raptor and owl placements are on a tree top;
- all 267 ground and water bird placements stand at 0 (the simulation has no
  ponds);
- the owl is drawn in the same 6% of night scenes, and a big bird in 45% of day
  scenes, as before. This changes where they stand, not which appear.

## What could not be verified here

- **Against photographs.** The shapes are primitives placed by the author's eye,
  checked in renders.
- **The field-guide lengths.** Recalled, as above; the test's 15% slack is for
  that.
- **On a real GPU.** Headless Chromium on SwiftShader.
- **A bird walking through a shrub.** The ground birds walk straight between
  their plants, and the viewer's collision check keeps them out of buildings, not
  plants. A grouse can cross a saskatoon's stems on the way to the next one.

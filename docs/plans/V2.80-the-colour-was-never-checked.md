# V2.80 — the colour was never checked

*Where 350 guessed flower colours came from, what a flora could fix, and where
Budd's ran out.*

## Where this started

One observation, from looking at a page:

> "I am looking at the plains prickly pear cactus which says it has a yellow
> flower whereas the picture on the page clearly shows a pink flower."

*Opuntia polyacantha* held `#f2c11e`, inherited from its genus, above a magenta
photograph. **350 of 430 colours were that**: a genus default marked *not
verified*, which is honest and is not the same as being right.

The end of it is that the prickly pear now publishes **"Yellow to orange"**,
because the species is variable and one hex could never say so.

## Two sources, one rejected

**USDA PLANTS was tried first** and it failed twice over. Its Characteristics
Search downloads the *result rows*, not the characteristics you filtered on, so
a plain export is a name list — worked around by downloading one colour at a
time and letting the filter be the label (`--colour-sets`, still implemented).
Then the data itself turned out wrong: *"They say yarrow is yellow, the giant
hyssop is red"*, checked at the binomial rather than the common name, so not
the *Achillea filipendulina* confusion it could have been. *A. millefolium* is
white. A source wrong about that is not a source.

**Budd's Flora of the Canadian Prairie Provinces** is the right authority for
this ground and has no colour column at all — it is a scanned book, and the
colour lives in sentences like *"Flowers white, rarely pinkish, in dense
flat-topped corymbs."*

## The design decision that mattered

**The module's job is not extraction. It is making 348 species checkable in an
evening.** OCR of a 1979 scan will be wrong sometimes and a parser reading
English will be wrong sometimes, so nothing is written unseen — instead every
proposal carries **the book's own sentence**, and a review row is decided by
reading a quote rather than by looking a species up.

That is what made the difference when the author said *"I do not want to sit
here and review everything"*: the answer was not a better sorting of the
questions, it was **removing the question**. 76 rows were being sent for
arbitration purely because a flora had named two colours, so multi-colour
became a thing the data model can hold (schema v81, `flower_colours`), and the
review dropped to 21.

## What the real book taught, in order

Each of these was invisible in a fixture and obvious in the scan.

**A compound is one colour, not two.** *"Flowers pinkish white"* is a white
tinged pink; only a connector makes a range. Reading compounds as ranges would
have had the site claim bearberry blooms "pink to white" where the book says
"pinkish white". 66 apparent ranges were really 45.

**A family heading ends a block.** Budd's runs them in capitals, which is not
Genus-then-epithet, so *Opuntia polyacantha* ran 968 characters into the
Elaeagnaceae and read their flowers too. It took the right sentence by luck.

**A 1979 flora does not use 2026 names.** *Cornus sericea* is absent; the book
files red osier dogwood under *Cornus stolonifera*. The common name crosses
that gap and is never treated as confident.

**The plant's own name orders a range.** Budd's gives *Coreopsis tinctoria*
"brownish disk florets and 6-10 broad yellow ray florets", so a word scanner
leads with brown — but the disk is the centre, the rays are the flower, and it
is called Golden Tickseed. `data_quality` caught that and *Dalea purpurea* as
errors before the rule existed. Nothing is added or dropped: the range is the
flora's, the order is the name's.

**`"budds"` is not a provenance mark.** It came back `recorded=False`, labelled
*"not recorded"* — which would have printed the opposite of the truth on 146
pages whose colour had just been sourced. `"flora"` already existed and means
exactly this.

**Colour buckets stopped being disjoint**, and four tests assumed otherwise. A
union of two colours is not a sum of two counts, and a row returned by a yellow
query need not have yellow as its *primary*.

**The render site had to be fixed too.** The species page drew
`COLOUR_LABELS[classify(row)]` while the entry carried "Yellow to orange", so
the prickly pear published as "Yellow" on the very build meant to fix it. Third
time that cell had been wrong the same way — re-deriving an answer the entry
already held — so it is now `colour_cell_html` with the history in its
docstring.

## Where Budd's ran out

The genus reading was built on a reasonable premise the author stated himself:
*"I'm okay with reading the genus if the genus colour is stated and species is
only stated for departures. This would hold for goldenrods but I'm unsure about
others."* The uncertainty was the point, so uniformity was **measured from the
book** — among the species of a genus that state their own colour, do they
agree? *Solidago* uniform, *Viola* variable, nobody's opinion involved.

It found **0 genus headings for the 80 genera** that needed them. What Budd's
carries under those names is identification key couplets (`7. Flowers purplish
blue, to 2 cm long Cassia`), figure captions, index entries and tribe headings.
**It is a key-plus-species-treatments flora with no genus paragraph.**

That made the 23 readings it did produce a liability: `Allium textile`, a
species heading whose authority the OCR dropped, matches the genus-heading
shape exactly and would lend one species' colour to all its siblings. So
`--genus` is off by default, kept rather than deleted because the code is sound
and the *source* is what fails.

One bug nearly hid all of this. Uniformity was measured over the species still
*needing* a colour — but those that state their own had been applied in the
previous pass and had left that list, so there was nothing to measure against
and all 23 came back "untested". **A test with an empty sample does not fail
loudly.** It stops testing and reports a confident-sounding verdict.

## Where it leaves the numbers

| | |
|---|---|
| Read from a flora (`flora`) | **146**, 45 of them a range |
| Checked against a name or Latin epithet | 35 |
| No colour recorded at all | 44 |
| Still a genus default (`estimated`) | **197** |

But 197 overstates the gap. **74 of them are wind-pollinated** — grasses,
sedges and rushes, which classify as `straw` from their *type*, for a botanical
reason no flora reading improves. The real remaining number is **123**.

## Next: Flora of North America

Budd's is exhausted. FNA is the candidate, and the work is small because
`budds_colour` reads prose and does not care whose prose it is.

**Why it is worth trying.** Published as web text rather than a scan, so no OCR
and no OCR errors. Descriptions carry colour in the same sentence form. It
treats every species here. And it is the modern standard, so its nomenclature
matches ours far better than a 1979 book's — the common-name fallback should
barely be needed.

**The one real objection, stated up front.** FNA is *continental*. A
description may span variation across a species' whole range rather than the
prairie form, which is the weakness that made USDA wrong. Two things make it
tolerable where USDA was not: FNA gives a full sentence rather than one
categorical value, and that sentence lands in the review file where it can be
read. A range that reads "white to purple across the continent" is visible as
such; USDA's bare "Purple" was not.

**How to do it, concretely.**

1. **Get the text.** FNA volumes are on the web per-taxon. Fetching ~123 pages
   is a scrape, so it needs a slow, polite fetcher of the kind
   `warm_photo_cache.py` already is — one request a second, resumable, caching
   to `data/fetched/`. This project's sessions cannot reach it, so the fetch is
   the author's and the parsing is the script's, as with VASCAN and Budd's.
2. **Strip the HTML.** Ten lines. `budds_colour.normalise` wants text with its
   line structure intact, so tags become newlines rather than spaces.
3. **Reuse everything else.** `colour_in`, the compound rule, the flower-word
   gate, the review sheet and `--from-review --apply` all work unchanged. The
   only new argument is a provenance mark — `flora` is right if the reading is
   species-level, which for FNA it always is.
4. **Check the same two species first.** *Achillea millefolium* must come back
   white and *Agastache foeniculum* blue. That test rejected USDA in one minute
   and it is the cheapest possible check on a new source.
5. **Expect the continental caveat to show up as ranges.** More multi-colour
   rows than Budd's gave, which the data model now holds. Whether a
   continent-wide range is the right thing to publish for a prairie catalogue
   is a decision to take *after* seeing how many there are, not before.

**What not to do.** Do not sample the catalogue's photographs for a dominant
hue. It is tempting and it is a different claim: it says *this photograph is
pink*, not *this species has pink flowers*, and one photograph of one plant in
one light is not a flora.

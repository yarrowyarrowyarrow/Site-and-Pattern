## What was actually wrong with the flight

Two faults, and the second is the one that mattered.

1. `flapWings` (`07-wildlife.js`) is a good function fed by hardcoded constants
   — `{ base: 0.2, amp: 0.75, speed: 0.09 }`. `speed` was a raw multiplier on
   the millisecond clock: **not hertz, not derived from anything**, and
   identical for every species in a taxon.
2. **The body path and the wings were completely decoupled.** A bird crossed
   the yard on `c.pos.lerp(tgt, dt * 3.0)` — a smooth exponential ease — no
   matter what its wings were doing. *That decoupling is the zipline.* You can
   beat the wings perfectly and the bird still slides, because nothing it does
   with them changes where it goes.

Fixing (1) alone would have produced correct numbers and an unchanged zipline.

## The model (`src/flight_model.py`)

### Birds — Pennycuick, and the correction that is *derived*

Pennycuick (1990, *J. Exp. Biol.* 150:171–185):

    f = m^(3/8) · g^(1/2) · b^(−23/24) · S^(−1/3) · ρ^(−3/8)

It **underestimates small passerines by about half**: an 11 g chickadee with a
203 mm span predicts ~12.6 Hz against a published 25–30 Hz. That is the formula
being used outside its derivation, which assumes **continuous flapping**.

Small passerines use **bounding flight** — a burst of beats, then the wings
fold and the bird travels ballistically. Wings working a fraction *d* of the
cycle must make 1/*d* times weight, and lift goes as f², so:

    f_burst ≈ f_continuous / √d

At d = 0.25 that is exactly the factor of two the measurements show. **The bout
structure is the reason for the rate**, which is why the two are computed
together rather than as two tuned tables — a "×2 fudge for small birds" would
get the same number and teach nothing.

**The distinction that must not be got wrong**, and which the first cut *did*
get wrong: the correction applies **only where the wings fold**. A gliding hawk
holds its wings extended, so they carry it throughout and there is nothing to
compensate for. Correcting it overpredicted a red-tailed hawk at 7.2–12.5 Hz
against a published 2.6–3.0, while the *uncorrected* formula gave 4.0. There is
a test.

Validated against nine published species — **eight of nine overlap**:

| | predicted | published |
|---|---|---|
| Black-capped Chickadee | 19.9–26.8 | 25–30 |
| American Goldfinch | 17.9–24.2 | 20–25 |
| Downy Woodpecker | 16.5–22.3 | 15–18 |
| American Robin | 8.6–14.4 | 12–14 |
| Blue Jay | 6.6–11.0 | 6–8 |
| Black-billed Magpie | 4.7–7.8 | 5–6 |
| Ruffed Grouse | 7.9–13.1 | 10–12 |
| Red-tailed Hawk | 3.0–4.9 | 2.6–3 |
| **Great Horned Owl** | **3.7–6.1** | **2.5–3** ← miss |

The owl is a real outlier — exceptionally low wing loading — and the right
answer is to **ship the measurement, not tune a constant until one species
fits**. `MEASURED_HZ` exists for exactly that, and a test asserts the formula
still misses it (so the override cannot rot into redundancy unnoticed).

### Insects — inverse size allometry on named anchors

Greenewalt (1962): within a group, frequency falls with inverse wing length.
`f = k · L^(−0.75)` for bees (anchor: *Apis mellifera* ≈230 Hz at 13 mm →
predicts *Bombus* 166 Hz vs published 150–160); `f = k/L` for Lepidoptera,
where the anchors fit first-power almost exactly (monarch ≈5.4 Hz at 100 mm,
cabbage white ≈11 Hz at 45 mm).

Below ~10 mm the bee fit is past both anchors, so it returns
`basis='extrapolated'` and a doubled band rather than a confident number.

**`flight_style` finally does something.** The schema-v58 column has reached
the viewer since V2.36 and been read by nothing. The first cut matched
substrings like "fast" and "swift" — **words that appear nowhere in the data**,
so it silently did nothing for 31 of 31 species. It is now an exact map over
the real closed vocabulary (`bobbing` 8, `gliding` 7, `erratic` 7, `darting` 3,
`hovering` 3, `fluttery` 3), with a test that fails if the data grows a value
the map does not handle. Skippers got their own constant — Hesperiidae are the
buzzy exception and are already a separate `kind` in the catalogue.

### Rendering — the honest limit

At 60 fps, Nyquist is 30 Hz. A bumblebee at 166 Hz animated at its true rate is
the **wagon-wheel effect**: a slow backwards flutter that is confidently wrong.
Those return `render='blur'` and the viewer holds the wing at mid-sweep,
translucent and widened — which is also all anybody has ever actually seen of a
bee's wings. P5, not a compromise.

## The data (`data/bird_morphology_master.json`, schema v63)

Birds were the **only flying taxon with no attributes table at all** — bees have
`body_length_mm` (69/69), Lepidoptera have wingspan + flight style (31/31), and
the 24 birds had nothing, which is why the wingbeats were hardcoded.

**⚠ Sourcing honesty.** This session had **no network access** (verified: all
outbound HTTPS returns `000`), so AVONET could not be fetched. Masses and
wingspans are from published literature — Dunning's *CRC Handbook of Avian Body
Masses* (2nd ed., 2008) and standard field-guide spans — but **could not be
checked against the primary sources as they were written**. Every row therefore
ships `verified = 0`, every row carries its citation, and a test asserts that
*nothing* claims verification. A session with egress should re-derive from
AVONET (Tobias et al. 2022, *Ecol. Lett.* 25:581, CC BY 4.0) and flip the flag.

`wing_area_cm2` is **null for every row** — the one bird measurement not
routinely published. The model infers it from span and a per-style aspect ratio
and will use a real figure the moment one is entered.

## What the viewer does with it (`html/scene3d/18-flight.js`)

* `hzToFlapSpeed(hz)` — one line converting hertz to the units `flapWings`
  already uses, which is why that function did not have to change at all.
* `beatGain(c, t)` — the bout envelope. Full amplitude through the burst,
  folded through the glide, eased at both ends because a bird finishes the
  stroke it is on.
* **`boundOffset(c, t)` — the zipline fix.** Metres of height, straight out of
  the bout: the bird climbs while the wings work and falls while they do not,
  ballistically if bounding and gently if gliding. Returned as an *offset*, so
  `07-wildlife.js` keeps owning the path and this only makes the path answer to
  the wings.
* The hover tip now shows the number and its basis, because a number nobody can
  see teaches nothing (P5) and "measured" vs "estimated" is a distinction that
  has to survive to the screen (P9).

Three small edits in `07-wildlife.js`, all `typeof`-guarded so the chunk is
purely additive.

## The third fault, found after shipping

The author, on the first build: *"Still not seeing wing flap on the butterflies
nor the birds."*

**Birds had a fourth thing wrong, and it made everything above invisible.**
`_GLB_CRITTER.bird` in `09-models.js` had **no `node` picker**. Every other
flying kind has one; the bird entry was missing it. Two consequences, both
silent:

1. `root` stayed the whole multi-variant file instead of one build, so every
   baked bird was drawn as **passerine + woodpecker + hummer superimposed**.
2. `prefix` stayed empty, so `byName('WingL')` looked for a bare `WingL` in a
   file whose nodes are `passerine_WingL` — found nothing — and left
   `userData.wings` unset. **`flapWings` returns immediately without it.**

So the baked bird could not move its wings at any frequency. The *procedural*
fallback sets wings correctly, which is why this only appeared once the GLBs
were loading, and why nothing in Python or the scene JSON could show it.

Correct wingbeat physics on an object with no wing pivots looks exactly like no
physics at all. That is the lesson worth keeping: **this increment's work was
untestable from the side I was working on.**

Two things came out of it:

* `tests/test_flight_model.py:TestTheWingsCanActuallyBeFound` **parses the real
  `.glb` files** and asserts the rule generally — *if a fauna model's wing nodes
  are variant-prefixed, its spec must carry a `node` picker*. Verified to fail
  on the pre-fix code with the exact diagnosis, and to pass after. A companion
  test checks every `build` name in `scene_wildlife` against the variants its
  model actually contains, so the next drift fails a test instead of quietly
  changing what an animal looks like.
* `window.permaFlightReport()` (and `permaFlightProblems()`) in `18-flight.js`
  report, per creature, whether it has wing pivots, flap params, its hertz, and
  its current gain. Diagnosing this from the outside cost a session; one call
  answers it now.

**Butterflies: no fault found.** Both their paths — baked and procedural — set
`userData.wings` and `userData.flap` correctly, all three lep builds
(`butterfly` / `skipper` / `swallowtail`) exist in `fauna_lep.glb`, and only 3
of 25 creatures in a test scene are single-waypoint (all beetles, which do not
flap). A travelling butterfly should be at full amplitude ~59% of its bout. If
they still look static after the bird fix, `permaFlightProblems()` is the thing
to run — I could not reproduce it from here and would rather say so than tune
something that may not be broken.

## The fourth fault — and it was the display, not the physics

The author, after the bird fix: *"The butterflies do move from point a to point
b, they simply have no flapping to their wings. They are horizontal and flat and
just glide along."* And: *"The birds move too fast for me to see what they are
doing but their flight still does not resemble a natural flight for a bird."*

This time I stopped reasoning and **ran the animation maths** in Node against a
stub `THREE`, sampling `pivot.rotation.z` frame by frame. The wings were moving
the whole time. The bug was that nothing could see them, and it was mine —
three compounding mistakes in the *display* half of the model:

1. **I calibrated to 60 fps. The view runs at 30.** `08-modes.js` caps the
   ambient loop at 30 fps and only lifts to 60 in walk / fly / cinematic mode.
2. **I used Nyquist, which is the wrong limit.** Two samples per cycle is
   enough to *reconstruct* a signal mathematically and nowhere near enough to
   *see* one. Roughly six frames per cycle is where repeating motion starts
   reading as motion. At 30 fps that is a **5 Hz ceiling** — below almost every
   bird in the catalogue. A 21 Hz goldfinch was getting **1.4 frames per
   cycle**: pure strobing, which is exactly "too fast to see what they are
   doing."
3. **The authored wing sweep was far too shy.** ~60° centred near horizontal —
   so even at a visible rate a butterfly read as *"horizontal and flat"*, which
   is precisely what was reported. A real lepidopteran brings its wings from
   near-vertical above the body to near-vertical below, about 140°.

### What changed

* **60 fps whenever wildlife is visible**, not just in first-person modes. The
  30 fps cap was set before anything in the scene beat its wings.
* **Three render regimes instead of two.** `discrete` (≤10 Hz, drawn at its
  true rate), `slowed` (≤45 Hz, animated at 10 Hz **and labelled as slowed**),
  `blur` (>45 Hz — bees, hummingbirds). A slowed beat keeps the rhythm, the
  bout and the shape of the stroke; a strobing one keeps nothing. The label
  admits it, the same way an estimate admits it is an estimate.
* **Wing sweep roughly doubled** for leps (60°→126°) and songbirds (52°→97°),
  in *both* flap tables, which have to agree.
* **Birds travel at constant speed.** `c.pos.lerp(tgt, dt * 3.0)` is an
  exponential ease — it launches the bird and decelerates into the perch, which
  nothing alive does. Speed halved as well (4.5 → 2.2 m/s): a chickadee really
  does cross ten metres in a second, but a yard is twenty metres wide and a
  creature gone before you can look at it teaches nothing. **That is a
  legibility choice and is labelled as one; the wingbeat stays exact**, with a
  test that fails if anyone "fixes" the flap by editing the physics.

Verified by simulation at the real 60 fps frame interval: butterfly sweep
2.20 rad over ~12 frames/cycle, bird 1.69 rad over 6 — both tracing clean arcs
instead of jumping. A test now asserts the general property: **whatever we
animate, there are at least six frames per cycle to show it in.**

### The lesson, again

Three rounds of this bug and every one was in a different layer: the physics
(wing-folding scope), the render contract (missing `node` picker → no wing
pivots), and the display limit (wrong frame rate, wrong threshold, too-small
sweep). None of them were visible from Python, and the first two were invisible
on screen except as an absence.

**Working on the Python side of a renderer means the work is untestable from
where you are standing.** The Node harness that finally settled it took ten
minutes and should have been the first thing, not the fourth.

### What the run said

**2,636 tests, OK, 10 skipped** — with PyQt6, PyQt6-WebEngine, the Qt runtime
libs and the scientific deps installed, so nothing widget-shaped skipped
silently. The 10 are `bpy` (Blender), a root-permissions case, and one
import-order guard. Zero Qt or WebEngine skips. `test_undo_redo` is excluded
and still segfaults in this GPU-less container, as the runbook describes.

Two of the increment's own tests caught real mistakes while it was being
written, both recorded above: the wing-folding scope (a threefold hawk
overprediction) and the `flight_style` substring matching that did nothing for
31 of 31 species. Neither would have been visible by looking at the animation.

**What I could not verify here:** the animation itself. Nobody has watched a
chickadee bound across a yard or a bee's wings blur — that needs a real desktop
with a GPU. The *numbers* are tested against published measurements and the
wiring is tested as text, but the frames have never been drawn. Nor could the
bird morphology be checked against its primary sources, for the reason above.

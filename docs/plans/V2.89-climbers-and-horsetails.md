# V2.89 — Climbers and horsetails

*Two families from batch D of the V2.87 model audit, done together: **F179**
climbers, which the owner's decision unblocked, and **F176** horsetails, the
cheapest body on the audit's list. Ten species between them, which is still one
contact sheet. The owner's instruction, verbatim:*

> *"Vines should climb adjacent plant but only if it is a tree or shrub"*

## F179 — Climbers

**What the audit found.** All six vines (Blue Clematis, Wild Clematis, Twining
Honeysuckle, Cream-Coloured and Purple Peavine, Wild Vetch) are drawn as
free-standing leafy columns their recorded length tall: a 6 m Wild Clematis is a
6 m green pillar standing in the open. A vine's recorded height is how far it
climbs, not how tall it stands.

**The rule, as implemented.** A vine climbs the tree or shrub it is planted
beside; with no tree or shrub beside it, it has nothing to climb and lies on the
ground. "Beside" means the two footprints touch: the gap between the vine's root
and the host's crown edge is no more than the vine's own spread radius (plus
10 cm). Where several qualify, the nearest crown edge wins, then the lower index,
so one design always draws the same way.

- **Hosts are trees and shrubs only.** A vine next to a wildflower, a grass or
  another vine sprawls. That is the owner's rule; in the field a vetch will
  scramble through tall grass, and this draws it on the ground instead.
- **Existing trees count.** An existing tree on the site is a tree.
- **A host has to be there.** A tree the succession has shaded out (`dead`) or
  one not yet present (opacity below 0.25, the viewer's own "not present" line)
  holds nothing up.
- **How high:** the vine's own length (its recorded height, grown to this
  year) or the host's height, whichever is less. A 3 m Blue Clematis on a 15 m
  spruce covers the lower 3 m of one side; on a 2 m saskatoon it reaches the
  top.
- **With nothing to climb:** a low tangle of trailing stems over its spread, no
  taller than 30 cm.

**Where the decision is made: Python, once.** `src/vine_habit.py` (new, Qt-free)
decides each vine's habit while `build_scene` assembles the scene and writes it
into the vine's record as a `drawn` block: habit, where the foliage centres,
how high it reaches, how wide it is, and for a climber the host it holds. The
viewer draws from that block and `scene_wildlife` perches animals on it. That is
the V2.88 lesson (A6): when the flowers and the body each worked out a plant's
height for themselves, the flowers floated. One number, read by both.

**Fitting the vine to the crown that is actually drawn.** A host's crown is a
baked model scaled to the species, not an ideal shape, and an ideal shape is
wrong where it matters: at 40% of a spruce's height its cone is 60% of the full
radius, while an ellipsoid of the same height and width is nearly 100%. Vine
leaves placed on the ellipsoid would hang in the air a metre off the tree. So
the viewer measures the host's own geometry once per archetype (branches and
foliage together): its outer radius at each height band and bearing. Stems run
from the vine's root to the host and climb its surface on the vine's side,
wandering around it; leaves sit on that surface, facing out.

**The leaves are the species' own.** The outline the catalogue records
(compound pinnate for the clematises, peavines and vetch; elliptic for the
honeysuckle) at the recorded leaf size, built by the same procedural leaf
builders the herbs use. **Flowers and fruit sit on the vine's leaves**, not in a
disc around its root: the floret, billboard and fruit layers read the vine's
anchor points when it has them.

**The Sprite Gallery shows both habits.** A vine's form now depends on its
neighbour, so a lone specimen would only ever show the sprawl. Each vine entry
shows the vine climbing a shrub beside it, and a second copy in the open.

**Left alone, on purpose:**
- The design generator does not place vines beside trees or shrubs. A generated
  vine with nothing beside it sprawls, which is true to the rule. Placing them
  well is a design rule, not a drawing one: recorded as a follow-up.
- Vines do not climb structures, fences or buildings. The rule names trees and
  shrubs.
- The host is not changed: a heavy vine does not pull a crown out of shape.
- The 2D map is unchanged.

## F176 — Horsetails

**What the audit found.** The four horsetails (*Equisetum arvense*, *fluviatile*,
*hyemale*, *variegatum*) are recorded as three different plant types (herb,
aquatic, rush), so they were drawn as a grass tuft, a reed tuft and two rush
tufts. **Two of them wore grass seed-head plumes**: Common Scouring-rush and
Variegated Horsetail are recorded `flower_form: plume` with the graminoid
straw colour `#cbbd80`, and a missing bloom period falls back to a June to
September window. A horsetail has no flowers at all; it makes spores in a cone.

**Decisions.**
- **A new growth form, `jointed`,** recorded on all four. The body follows the
  habit, not the habitat: `plant_type` stays herb, aquatic and rush, because it
  drives the layers, the filters and the website. `emergent` was also wrong for
  Common Horsetail, which is a plant of fields and ditch banks, not of standing
  water. `jointed` is true of every *Equisetum* by definition, so this is not a
  guess about any one species.
- **`stem_branching` recorded**: Common Horsetail `branched_throughout` (the
  sterile shoot's regular whorls are what the name describes), Swamp Horsetail
  `branched_above` (it varies: unbranched, or whorled at the middle nodes),
  Common Scouring-rush and Variegated Horsetail `unbranched`.
- **The plumes go**: `flower_form` `none` and no flower colour for the two that
  had them, matching the other two.
- **Provenance stays `estimated`.** Both Flora of North America sites
  (floranorthamerica.org and efloras.org) are blocked from this container, so
  none of these values is claimed as read from a flora. The branching values
  are the standard descriptions of these species, and the gate's rule that a
  `flora` claim must cite what it read is exactly why they are not marked as one.
- **Drawn, not baked.** A horsetail is a clump of jointed cylinders: dark
  sheaths at the nodes, whorls of thin branches or none, a pointed tip on the
  unbranched ones. The procedural path is the one Stylised draws anyway, and a
  baked copy would be a second definition of the same geometry to keep in step.
  Seed heads and florets are drawn procedurally for the same reason.
- **Schema v89**, because the seed data changed.

**Left alone:** the cones themselves (a pointed tip stands in for them on the
unbranched species); Common Horsetail's brown fertile spring stems, which wither
before the green shoots are up; the spreading, which is the existing colony
model.

## What changed on the way

Seven things the plan above did not foresee, all found by looking at renders.

1. **The profile could not see a trunk.** The first measurement of the host
   read its vertices. A trunk is a long cylinder with vertices only at its two
   ends, so on a Trembling Aspen the profile was empty between 0.4 m and 6.8 m
   and the climb found nothing to hold above 1.6 m. The profile now samples
   along every triangle edge. The aspen's trunk reads as a continuous 0.26 to
   0.35 m radius over that whole span, and the vine climbs it.
2. **Rise, or run and rise.** The first version ran every stem along the soil
   to the host's foot and then straight up it. The result looked like a bean
   pole: long bare runs across the ground and a vertical ribbon. Now two stems in
   three rise from the root and lean in to meet the host where its crown comes
   within the vine's spread, no higher than 0.8 of the climb. The third runs
   along the ground, wandering, and climbs from the foot. On the host each stem
   sways around it instead of rising on one line, and it may not leap outward
   faster than it climbs. A stem that meets the base of a crown works out
   through the lower branches rather than jumping to the leaf surface.
3. **Side shoots.** Three to nine stems covered a shrub as a few ribbons with
   bare crown between them. Every two or three samples up a stem, a lateral
   shoot 0.25 to 0.6 m long now runs across the host's surface. Leaves sit a
   little over half a leaf-length apart along every stem. That took a shrub
   from a few bare ribbons to leafy strands across its side, still well short
   of the real plant's mass (see *Found, not fixed*). One vine is capped at
   1,200 leaves, so a 6 m Wild Clematis costs no more than a small tree.
4. **Blooms off the ground.** When every leaf was an anchor, the first renders
   put flowers at the root, on the runners and half into the soil. Anchors are
   now every third leaf. On a climber they are taken only above a third of the
   climb, and never on a ground run. The floret layer also keeps a head at
   least 2 cm above the ground.
5. **`layer.vine` retired rather than rebaked.** The baked column (its GLB, its
   Blender builder, its manifest entry, its aspect break) and the procedural
   column in `14-layers.js` are deleted. A climbing vine is fitted to its host
   in each scene, and a model baked once cannot be. `tests/test_model_assets.py`
   now expects three layer models.
6. **Where the vine is.** For a climber the `drawn` position is the centre of
   its foliage (three quarters of the way from the host's centre to its crown
   edge, on the vine's side), not its root. That is where the animals perch.
7. **The gallery's vine hangs on the shrub's right-hand edge.** Due east of
   the dogwood, the first layout, the climbing copy was half behind it. Turned
   to face the camera, it was lost among the shrub's own leaves. On the edge of
   the shrub's silhouette as the gallery camera sees it (east-north-east), the
   clematises and the honeysuckle read as vines hanging on a shrub. The
   peavines and vetch climb about a metre, under the dogwood's overhang, and
   stay hard to see from the gallery's distance. One-metre hosts (snowberry,
   currant, spirea) hid them just as well, so the dogwood stays and the test
   plan says to zoom in.

  only, adjacency, the nearest host, a dead or absent host, an existing tree,
  the climb and sprawl heights, and animals perching on the drawn vine.
  `tests/test_horsetails.py` (6) covers the data. The render probe gains four
  rendered cases and one source check:
  - the climber's leaves reach at least 0.6 of the climb, never more than 0.2 m
    above it, and extend in over the host's crown;
  - the lone vine stays under 0.42 m;
  - horsetails stand at 0.85 to 1.25 of their recorded height;
  - a horsetail is one body, with no plume.

  - `tests/test_flower_colour.py` (two) pins how many species fall in each

  - `tests/test_skill_library.py` checks the schema version the skills

- **Renders** (headless Chromium, software GL):
  - On the Red Osier Dogwood, the clematises and the honeysuckle hang in leafy

  - On a Black Spruce the vines cover the lower branches of one side; the

## Found, not fixed

- **The foliage is thinner than the plant.** A Wild Clematis in August is a
  solid mantle over the shrub it climbs, and this draws a lattice of leafy
  strands. The structure is right (on the neighbour, to its length, on the
  vine's side) and the mass is not. Wild Clematis already sits at the
  1,200-leaf cap, so thickening it means bigger or clustered leaves, not
  more of them. It belongs with the flower data below: both are about how
  much of the vine there is, not where it goes.
- **Vine flowers are genus defaults.** All six vines record
  `flowering_stems: 14`, a banded value 78 species share, with
  `flower_data_source: estimated`. On a vine that now covers the side of a shrub,
  14 heads read sparse. Wild Clematis also carries Blue Clematis's flower:
  `solitary`, `bell`, 3 cm. Standard descriptions give it many-flowered
  clusters of small, open, white flowers in the leaf axils. It needs reading
  from a flora, and both Flora of North America sites are blocked here, so it
  is recorded rather than changed.
- **The forage calendar counts every grass as bee forage.**
  - The cause: `forage_calendar._has_flowers` counts any `flower_form` other
    than `none`. Every graminoid is `plume` on purpose, because that column
    feeds the pollinator logic.
  - The effect: all 78 grasses, sedges and rushes count as forage, each with
    the June to September fallback window. The function's own docstring says
    they must not. (It was 80 when first counted: two of those "rushes" were
    the horsetails, which this increment took off `plume`.)
  - Where it shows: the docent's narration. The habitat score is not affected.
  - Found while checking what the horsetails' plumes fed. Left as its own
    increment: the fix is a pollinator decision, not a drawing one.
- **`04-quality.js` is at 892 lines of its 900.** V2.89 added 30 lines, for
  the horsetail routing and the host notes. The next change there has to move
  something out first, as V2.34 did with `14-layers.js`.

## What could not be verified

- **A real desktop.** Every render here is software GL in a headless browser,
  and the app's own 3D Preview window was not opened.
- **The flora values.** The horsetail branching and the Wild Clematis
  observation above rest on standard descriptions, not on a flora read in this
  session. That is why the horsetail values stay `estimated`.
- **How often it happens in a real design.** The generator does not yet put
  vines beside trees or shrubs, so outside a hand-placed design most vines will
  sprawl.

## The release first: PR #11, and what it does not do

`main` was at **V2.72** (PR #10, 20 August). PR #11 brings it to V3.37 at
`3a00dbd`: tests green, both installers built from that commit, and GitHub
reported the PR mergeable and clean (263 commits). **Merging a PR does not
make a release.** Since V3.18 every push publishes a *prerelease*, which
Help › Check for Updates skips. Offering V3.37 to installed copies is a
separate step, the **promotion**: Edit on the release page (untick *Set as a
pre-release*, tick *Set as the latest release*), or Actions › *Promote a
release* › `V3.37` once the merge has put `promote-release.yml` on `main`.
GitHub lists a hand-run workflow only from the default branch, so that
workflow cannot be used until the merge.

Two things the merge fixes on its own, both found in this investigation:

- **`main`'s README is the V2.72 one.** It still says *PolyForm
  Noncommercial*, which has been wrong since V3.18 (GPL-3.0), on the page
  every visitor to the repository sees first.
- **Help › How to Plan Your Yard pointed at a section `main` does not have.**
  See G below.

One cosmetic mismatch stays: the `release-V3.37` *tag* points at `d93e97f`,
the branch's first V3.37 push. `softprops/action-gh-release` creates a tag
once and never moves it. The installers on the release were rebuilt from
`3a00dbd` and are current; only the page's auto-generated "Source code"
archives are from the older commit. Moving the tag would mean a force-push of
a published tag, which is the owner's call and buys nothing the updater uses.

**All new work goes on V3.38 and later**, so V3.37 stays exactly the build
that was tested. (The V3.37 sessions committed three later increments onto
V3.37, as their harness told them; the V3.37 choosing-and-naming plan noted
that a V3.38 would be offered by the updater and V3.37 would not.)

### V3.39 · The walk teaches one plant at a time (F255)

The owner's shape, nearly word for word: *this is this plant, here is what it
does, here are the creatures it supports; click an animal and see the plants
it relies on, here and not here yet.*

- **The plant card.** Photograph and credit; name; one sentence on what it
  does in this landscape ("Feeds 63 kinds of animals here: caterpillars of 31
  moths and butterflies, 18 bees, 9 birds…"); the animals that would lose
  their only food here if it were pulled. Then **the creatures it supports,
  led by at most eight a person can get to know**: an English name first,
  then a photograph, then recorded near the place, then out in the scene now.
  Each row says how the animal uses the plant ("eats the berries",
  "caterpillars eat the leaves") and opens the animal's card. The rest are
  folded by group with a count ("and 41 more: 22 small bees, 12 moths, 7
  flies, most known only by a Latin name"), one click away.
- **The animal card.** Photograph where one exists; name and group; its
  sentence; the months it is out; **the plants it relies on: here** (planted in
  this landscape, ticked) **and not here yet** (native around the place, best
  picks first), with *Plant one* beside any that the walk's palette offers.
  *Plant one* arms the trowel with that plant.
- **Back and forward** between cards, so plant → animal → plant reads as a
  path; Esc closes.
- **"Who lives here" stops being a list of everything.** *Identify* labels
  only the creatures tied to the selected plant (or, with none selected, the
  few nearest you). A small "26 out now ▸" opens the creatures in the scene as
  a clickable list, because a moving bee is hard to click. The "supports 470"
  headline moves into the plant and landscape lines, where it means something.
- **The first hint** becomes "Click a plant to see who it feeds."
- **Each walk names its species (row 14).** A community lists the species
  that define it, defining ones first (Trembling Aspen in the parkland;
  White Spruce and Trembling Aspen in the boreal mixedwood), with the genera
  kept only as a fallback for a species the catalogue lacks. Each list is
  checked against a published description of the natural region
  (Natural Regions Committee 2006 for Alberta) and recorded with it.
- **Planted the way they grow:** trees in clumps (aspen spreads as clones),
  shrubs in thickets and along the grove's edge, forbs and grasses in
  drifts, from rules rather than a fixed layout (P1), so the walk looks
  grown, not scattered.

Photographs: plants mostly have one; animals mostly do not (58 of 1,144 have
a credited photograph, 7 of 338 bees). Until F257 an animal without a photo
shows its group's drawing, not an empty frame.

### V3.40 · The score, and features, in the walk (F256)

- **Two numbers above the walk, each with its change after every plant, pull
  or feature: the animals this landscape feeds, and the Habitat Value
  Score** (the same score Design's pages show,
  `habitat_score.score_for_project`). Both, because row 15 shows the score
  alone would teach the wrong lesson: pulling the Chokecherry reads "animals
  fed 470 → 412, habitat score 81 (unchanged)", and the line under them says
  why the score held ("it counts kinds of plants doing each job, and 13 other
  plants here still feed birds"), which is redundancy, the thing that keeps
  a real community standing. *Why 81?* opens the score's seven parts. The
  score counts kinds, not individuals, and the walk says so the first time a
  second Saskatoon changes nothing. The Overview stays number-free (O1).
- **Features you can add and remove:** bee hotel, bee log, brush pile, snag,
  rock pile, pond, and two new ones: **leaf litter** (leaves left on the beds
  over winter) and **bare ground for nesting bees**. Drawn from the models
  the viewer already has; the two new ones as flat patches.
- **Each feature counts for what it does (D3).** The structure part stays 10
  of the 100 points, so no design can score more than before, but a type
  scores by its habitat value instead of a flat 2:

  | Feature | Points | Why |
  |---|---|---|
  | Pond (shallow, planted edge) | 4 | Water draws the widest range of animals of any one feature: dragonflies, frogs and toads, water insects, and birds that drink and bathe |
  | Snag (standing dead tree) | 3 | Cavity-nesting birds and the beetles and fungi of dead wood; nothing else in a yard replaces it |
  | Brush pile | 3 | Winter cover for juncos, sparrows and chickadees, shelter for small mammals and overwintering insects |
  | Leaf litter *(new)* | 3 | Where many moth and butterfly caterpillars and pupae, bumble bee queens, beetles and spiders spend the winter |
  | Bare ground for nesting bees *(new)* | 3 | Most native bees nest in the ground, in bare, sunny, undisturbed soil |
  | Bee log | 2 | Nest holes for wood- and stem-nesting bees |
  | Rock pile | 2 | Warm, dry shelter for insects and spiders |
  | Rain garden | 2 | Water for a day or two after rain, and a place for wet-ground plants (the plants count separately) |
  | Bioswale | 1 | Mostly drainage |
  | Native lawn patch | 1 | Better than lawn, still mown |
  | Bee hotel | 1 | Used by some solitary bees, but also by introduced bees and wasps, and parasites and mould build up unless the tubes are cleaned or replaced each year (MacIvor & Packer 2015) |
  | Rain barrel | 0 | It waters the garden. It is not habitat |

  This changes the headline score of any design with a structure, the change
  the headline-stability rule reserves to the owner. The owner asked for it;
  the numbers are D3 and live in one table.

### V3.42 · Three lessons (F88, first part)

A first version of the text, for the owner to rewrite. Each lesson is a few
short pages, about 120–200 words each, in plain words, with one *Look at it*
pointer per page (the walk, or a page of your design) and a readout
**about a landscape you choose**: one of the walks, or one of your saved
designs. That replaces "taught against your design", which meant the
sandbox nobody had planted.

1. **Designing for biodiversity** (the four current lessons, rewritten as
   one): plants that feed the most (keystone plants, and who Doug Tallamy
   is); the food web from plant to caterpillar to bird; one plant, many
   neighbours (relationships, not objects); plants change over time
   (quick starters and long-lived plants); features that shelter (structures,
   and which do the most); ranges, not certainties (why the numbers are
   estimates, and how to check them outside).
2. **Site factors to consider in design:** sun and shade through the day and
   the year; water (how fast it drains, where it collects, roof runoff); soil
   (texture, pH, don't enrich it for prairie natives); slope and contour; wind
   and snow (drifts, and where the shovelled snow and road salt go along a
   sidewalk); frost dates and zone; what is already there (trees, buildings,
   utilities, and calling before you dig).
3. **Aesthetic considerations:** three ways to lay a bed out (wild,
   structured, themed) and when each suits a street; height (tall at the back
   or see-through), layers and spacing; drifts and repetition; colour through
   the season, and foliage, seed heads and grasses in winter; cues to care, the
   mown edge, path or fence that tells a neighbour the wildness is on purpose
   (Nassauer 1995).

Sources per lesson, from `docs/REFERENCES.md` where the work is already there
(Tallamy 2007 and 2019, Rainer & West 2015, Nassauer 1995) and added there
when not. The lessons' module joins the P12 scan that already guards the plant
pages.

## Not doing, on purpose

- **No score on the Overview.** O1 stands; the walk is the learning side.
- **The docent script stays.** Only its page goes from Learn (and from Share
  on D4); the presentation stills and the API's `docent_script` still use it.

  the owner has used it.

  data job with sources, like F257.
